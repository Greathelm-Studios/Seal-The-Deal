CORE IDENTITY
01  ART-DIRECTION NORTH STAR

The project should look like an optimistic corporate future designed around 1999–2003 rather than a contemporary sci-fi world dressed in retro references. The visual language is clean, cool, sharply designed and intentionally artificial. The world is aspirational on the surface: white towers, blue skies, immaculate signage, glass, brushed metal and luminous office light. The game itself then abuses that immaculate world with slapstick PvP chaos.
The key contrast is deliberate: the environment is airy and architecturally clean, while characters are graphic, exaggerated and immediately readable. The result should feel like an early-2000s design agency built the city, a fashion illustrator designed the employees, and then somebody started a workplace war.
THE SHORTEST POSSIBLE BRIEF
2001 corporate futurism outside. Sharp graphic lawyers inside. Clean enough to feel expensive; strange enough to feel fictional; simple enough to read during chaos.

Reference 04 — PRIMARY environmental anchor: white-blue corporate city, immaculate massing, giant graphic branding, clear roads, cobalt sky and restrained cyan accents.
This image is the strongest single environment reference for the project. The most important lesson is not “make a futuristic city”; it is how little information each building needs. White slabs, dark window bands, one large blue graphic, tiny signage and clean spacing do most of the work.

COLOUR
03  COLOUR SYSTEM

The world should feel brand-managed. Colour is not decorative noise: it is a controlled system that makes the environment feel corporate, legible and intentionally designed. The palette should skew cool. Warmth is allowed when motivated by a specific prop, skin tone or gameplay accent, but it should not turn the project pastel or cozy.

Colour behaviour
■ White and pale grey should occupy the most visual area in architecture and interfaces.
■ Cobalt is structural: use it in large confident blocks rather than sprinkling small blue objects everywhere.
■ Cyan is an accent, not a base colour. It works best as a thin line, indicator, light strip or tiny digital emphasis.
■ Black/navy provide silhouette contrast and stop the palette becoming soft or pastel.
■ Character-specific or gameplay accents can sit on top of this system, but should be intentionally rationed.
COLOUR DANGER
Do not let “Y2K” become pink chrome, rainbow gradients, candy translucency or vaporwave. The desired Y2K is corporate, architectural and cold.

LIGHTING
04  LIGHTING &amp; ATMOSPHERE

Lighting is where the “airy” half of the style lives. The world can be soft, luminous and slightly hazy even while character shading remains hard-edged. This contrast is essential.
Exterior daylight
■ Prefer strong blue sky, pale atmospheric perspective and bright architectural whites.
■ Keep the skyline readable as large blocks; distant haze should simplify rather than add noise.
■ Glass can tint toward blue/cyan but must not become a mirror that hides cross-building gameplay.
■ Bloom is restrained. Bright edges may glow slightly, but the frame should remain crisp and graphic.
Interior office light
■ Cool fluorescent / LED-like illumination with broad soft ambient fill.
■ White surfaces should remain white or cool grey, not warm cream.
■ Harder directional accents can carve characters and props out of the soft ambient field.
■ Service rooms may be darker and more enclosed, but should still belong to the same cool corporate world.
■ Avoid dramatic cyberpunk coloured light unless a specific event or gameplay effect requires it.
LIGHTING FORMULA
SOFT WORLD LIGHT + HARD CHARACTER VALUE BREAKS. The environment supplies air; the character shader supplies graphic punch.

RENDERING
05  CEL-SHADING LANGUAGE

The character shader must not read as a generic modern anime toon shader. The project needs fewer bands, fewer effects and stronger decisions. The target is editorial illustration logic translated into 3D.
Core shader rule
BASE → CUT SHADOW
A material should usually read as one base field plus one major hard-edged shadow field. Additional shading is exceptional, not default.

■ One primary shadow threshold. A second threshold only when a material or face genuinely needs it.
■ No smooth character gradients as the default look.
■ Shadows may shift slightly colder rather than simply becoming black.
■ Highlights should appear as designed shapes: a hard streak on leather, a pale plane on hair, a sharp lapel reflection.
■ Outlines are selective. Use them to protect silhouette or clarify overlaps, not as a thick black border around every object.
■ Allow art-directed cheats. If a prettier shadow better explains the pose, silhouette or face, visual clarity wins over physical correctness.
Material-specific treatment

CHARACTERS
06  CHARACTER DESIGN SYSTEM

Characters should feel like fashion-forward corporate caricatures, not mascots. The agreed proportion language leans toward Jet Set Radio: compact torso, long legs and limbs, graphic hands and shoes, and a silhouette that can be recognised through a window from the opposite tower.
Proportion rules
■ Long legs and relatively compact torso.
■ Lean, nimble builds rather than chibi or bulky heroic anatomy.
■ Hands may be oversized enough to read animation and weapon poses clearly.
■ Shoes can be chunky or elongated as a graphic counterweight, but should still feel sleek rather than toy-like.
■ Heads and facial features stay stylised, but the whole character must not feel “cute.”
■ The male blazer should sit in a controlled fitted zone: not cropped to the waist, not oversized or slouchy.
■ The female silhouette can be pushed slightly for character, but not exaggerated into caricature that breaks the shared base style.
Shape language
■ Angular over rounded: pointed collars, triangular lapels, sharp shoulder cuts, clean crease lines and tapering shapes.
■ Use a few large clothing shapes rather than many decorative seams, buckles or panels.
■ Negative space between limbs matters. Avoid poses and silhouettes that collapse into one dark mass.
■ Each character should have one or two memorable silhouette decisions, not ten surface details.
READABILITY TEST
If a character becomes a 100-pixel-tall silhouette against a bright window, you should still understand their stance, major clothing shape and which way they are facing.

CORPORATE FASHION
07  CLOTHING &amp; WARDROBE LANGUAGE

The default wardrobe is corporate tailoring treated as graphic fashion design. It should look recognisably professional while being sharp enough to support the project’s stylisation.
■ Neutral suit templates form the baseline for player characters.
■ Jackets are clean and fitted with deliberate lapel geometry. Avoid both extreme crop and oversized contemporary streetwear proportions.
■ Trousers may flare or widen toward the shoe to reinforce the long-leg silhouette and late-90s/early-2000s influence.
■ Shirts, ties and belts should use strong simple shapes; tiny tailoring detail should not dominate.
■ Accessories should feel like corporate/fashion objects: glasses, watches, pins, ID cards, slim bags, earpieces — used sparingly.
■ Corporate styling should remain believable enough that the slapstick violence feels more absurd by contrast.
Secretary / lobby visual role
The secretary belongs to the same visual world but should read as an intentional front-of-house silhouette: glasses, no tie, a slightly unbuttoned top, clean office tailoring and a more composed stance. The design should distinguish the role without turning into a separate art style.
Customisation principle
Customisation should swap clear graphic categories — hair mass, eyewear, tie/shirt treatment, jacket variation, trousers, shoes and a small number of accessories — while preserving the base silhouette system. Avoid custom parts that introduce excessive visual noise or break cross-building readability.
STYLISATION DETAILS
08  HANDS, FACES &amp; ANATOMY SIMPLIFICATION

Face language
■ Large, readable eyes with heavier upper lids rather than rounded “cute” eyes.
■ Expressions lean detached, tired, cocky, annoyed or self-assured rather than bubbly.
■ Mouths can be absent or extremely reduced in the base style; eyes, brows, lids and head angle carry most expression.
■ Faces should be sharper and more angular than earlier soft/cute tests.
■ Avoid dense anime facial rendering, glossy lips, detailed noses or soft baby-face proportions.
Hand construction
The hand design is intentionally simplified for modelling and animation. It should read like a graphic cartoon hand rather than a realistic five-finger hand.
■ Three primary masses/joints: thumb, index finger, and a single joined mass representing the middle/ring/pinky group.
■ The joined three-finger mass must still imply three fingers through three subtle bumps/peaks in the silhouette.
■ Hands should not look puffy or mitten-like.
■ Avoid making the fingers long and elegant; the hand should stay compact, graphic and easy to read in action.
■ This simplification should carry consistently across male and female base models.
ENVIRONMENT
09  ENVIRONMENT ARCHITECTURE

The environment is not “cyberpunk office.” It is an idealised corporate future that never happened: clean, white, blue, elegant and slightly empty. Architecture should feel closer to commercial/transport design and turn-of-the-millennium concept renders than to gritty sci-fi.
Massing rule
LARGE FORMS FIRST
Build the room or facade from a few confident masses. Add graphic colour zones and signage second. Add small construction detail only where it improves scale, function or gameplay readability.

■ White or pale-grey slabs, dark window bands, cobalt panels and blue-tinted glass are the core vocabulary.
■ Curves can appear, but should be clean architectural curves rather than bubbly “aero” shapes.
■ Use bold facade graphics: a single blue rectangle across multiple floors is stronger than dozens of tiny blue props.
■ Signage can be oversized and integrated into the building skin.
■ External roads, bridges and infrastructure should feel neat and graphic rather than dirty or industrial.
■ Negative space is valuable. Do not fill every wall, corner or skyline gap.
World tone
The ideal impression is “a multinational company’s 2001 vision of 2025.” It should feel optimistic, efficient and expensive — then become ridiculous once players begin shooting through the windows and throwing office equipment.
INTERIORS
10  INTERIOR OFFICE LANGUAGE

The towers should combine idealised corporate futurism with recognisable office banality. The futuristic shell becomes more believable when it contains mundane real-world objects and slightly ugly workplace materials.
The beautiful layer
■ Glass meeting rooms and partitions.
■ White wall planes and pale modular ceiling systems.
■ Brushed aluminium, chrome details and blue-grey tinted glass.
■ Large geometric wall graphics and corporate identifiers.
■ Clean reception desks, elevator halls and long window lines.
■ Sparse cyan status lights and electronic displays.
The mundane layer
■ Grey office carpet and acoustic fabric.
■ Cheap ceiling tiles and fluorescent fixtures.
■ Printers/copiers, paper trays and filing storage.
■ Corded office phones, older monitors or early-flat-panel tech depending on department.
■ Water coolers, bins, stationery, office chairs and conference furniture.
■ Break-room and service-room equipment that looks practical rather than futuristic.
WHY THE MUNDANE MATTERS
Do not beautify every prop. The comedy is stronger when a pristine future contains the same annoying photocopier, carpet tiles and meeting chairs as a normal office.

GAMEPLAY READABILITY
12  WINDOWS, SIGHTLINES &amp; CROSS-TOWER COMBAT

Windows are not background dressing; they are part of the combat language. The art direction must support the “window wars” premise by letting teams track each other across the gap almost constantly.
■ Players must silhouette cleanly against windows and interior light.
■ Glass transparency and reflection need to be tuned for visibility first, realism second.
■ Meeting rooms can use glass walls so opponents can see activity through multiple layers.
■ Service rooms can be fully opaque. Hiding there should intentionally trade enemy visibility for the player’s own loss of information.
■ The main stair entrance at the centre-back of the floor should remain visually legible so opponents can understand when a team is progressing upward.
■ Window mullions, blinds and furniture placement must not create constant visual bars across character silhouettes.
■ Across-building architecture should frame combat rather than compete with it.
Floor readability
Open office floors should remain visually open/interconnected. Use furniture clusters and glass rooms to create local cover while preserving long sightlines. Dense corridor mazes work against the project’s identity and should be reserved for specific service/back-of-house moments rather than becoming the default.
GRAPHIC DESIGN
13  SIGNAGE, TYPOGRAPHY &amp; CORPORATE GRAPHICS

The graphic system should feel like it was designed at the same time as the buildings. UI, signs, posters, elevator numbers, floor maps and branding should share a turn-of-the-millennium corporate-tech grammar.
Core graphic motifs
■ Thin hairline grids and alignment rules.
■ Large cobalt rectangles or bands placed with confidence.
■ Small cyan squares, status lights and marker elements.
■ Oversized floor numbers and department identifiers.
■ Tiny strings of legal/technical microtype.
■ Crop marks, serial numbers, barcodes and form-like alignment cues used sparingly.
■ Circles, rectangles and abstract geometric overlays rather than decorative illustration.
■ Monochrome or cool-tinted fashion/corporate photography on billboards and internal campaigns.
■ Large blank areas; information should feel intentionally placed, not packed.

INTERFACE
14  UI / HUD DIRECTION

The interface should feel like an extension of the same corporate operating system seen in signage and architecture. Persona is useful here as a design philosophy — not because the UI should look like Persona, but because every menu, transition and label should belong to the game’s visual identity.
■ Use clean white/blue space, thin line systems, geometric frames and restrained transparency.
■ Favor strong typographic hierarchy over decorative panels.
■ Corporate/legal language is played straight. System names can sound mundane, procedural or contractual rather than videogame-fantasy.
■ Important alerts can use cobalt/electric blue or a deliberately reserved warning colour; normal UI should stay quieter.
■ UI animation should feel precise and designed: sliding alignment, snapping lines, form fields, document-like reveal — not bubbly app motion.
■ Menus can reference forms, contract clauses, floor directories and corporate intranet systems without becoming literal skeuomorphic office software.
HUD noise budget
Because players are already tracking enemies through windows, moving props, gunfire and vertical progression, the HUD should not cover large sections of the frame. Use small high-contrast information blocks and reserve big graphics for transitions, results screens and lobby presentation.
PROPS
15  PROPS &amp; TECHNOLOGY

Props are where the project can sell its alternate 2001-future most convincingly. The goal is not to make every stapler sci-fi. Instead, combine recognisable office objects with a small number of unusually clean or optimistic design decisions.
■ Phones, monitors, printers and terminals can sit between late-90s corporate hardware and optimistic near-future product design.
■ Use pale plastics, dark smoked screens, blue indicator lights and brushed metal rather than RGB lighting.
■ Office props should remain chunky enough to read when thrown, shot or knocked across a room.
■ Loose props can take more personality than architecture because they participate in slapstick destruction.
■ Paper, folders and stationery should reinforce the legal/corporate theme through labels, stamps, contract formatting and firm branding.
■ Avoid turning the office into a museum of nostalgia. The setting is a future imagined from that era, not literally an office frozen in 2001.

FEEDBACK
17  VFX &amp; MOMENT-TO-MOMENT FEEDBACK

Detailed VFX styling is still a production-test area, but effects must obey the same visual economy as the rest of the game. They should clarify action without burying the clean architecture in constant particles.
■ Smoke should read as a large soft volume that temporarily interrupts sightlines rather than a glittery particle cloud.
■ Muzzle flashes and impact effects should be crisp, brief and high-contrast.
■ Explosions can be exaggerated for slapstick impact, but debris and particles should decay quickly enough that window visibility returns.
■ Use the established blue/cyan system for corporate/interface feedback; do not recolour every combat effect blue if the real-world material suggests otherwise.
■ Screen-space effects should remain sparse and avoid heavy chromatic aberration, VHS noise or retro filters as a permanent look.
PRODUCTION TEST
VFX treatment is not fully locked. Prototype for readability first, then stylise within the blue-white corporate visual system.

ENVIRONMENT PRODUCTION
18  MODULAR ASSET HANDOFF RULES

The building uses modular environment pieces and authored room sets that can support randomized floor assembly. Art assets therefore need consistent dimensions, pivots, material families and visual grammar. The goal is for procedural variation to feel like different departments of one coherent corporation, not randomly assembled asset packs.
Asset-set rules
■ Build the fixed architectural kit first: wall bays, window bays, floor/ceiling modules, columns, stair connections, glass partitions and service-wall pieces.
■ Design room sets as authored compositions in Unity after the base modular pieces exist. This gives the coder clear reusable chunks while preserving art-directed layouts.
■ Keep door, window, ceiling and structural heights consistent across sets unless a special floor intentionally breaks the rule.
■ Use a small shared material library. Variation should come from arrangement, graphic signage and selected props more than dozens of unique materials.
■ Furniture should snap to sensible placement zones but still allow loose objects for physics and destruction.
■ Every room set should be evaluated from both its own tower and the opposing tower. Cross-window readability is part of the art review, not only level design.
Visual hierarchy for modular pieces
A module is successful when it still looks intentional in multiple combinations. Keep the base architecture quiet; reserve distinctive graphics and hero props for authored room-set layers. This prevents procedural assembly from creating accidental visual clutter.
PRESENTATION
19  COMPOSITION &amp; MARKETING IMAGERY

Key art, menus, character cards and promotional graphics should use the same negative-space discipline as the reference material. A character does not need to fill the frame. Empty blue or white space, hairline grids and small blocks of data can make the image feel more expensive and more specific to the project.
■ Use large crop decisions: characters can sit small in a huge field when the composition supports it.
■ Combine clean renders with thin line geometry and sparse editorial labels.
■ Photography-like billboards and character portraits can be cool-toned, monochrome or partially desaturated.
■ Use lens flare rarely and intentionally as an early-digital/future-camera motif, not as a constant effect.
■ Avoid busy collage layouts unless a screen specifically needs high energy. The default identity is controlled, not maximalist.
Title treatment
The main title should dominate with a sharp, confident corporate presence. The subtitle “Terms and Conditions Apply.” works as the dry legal counterpoint: smaller, secondary, almost like fine print. Avoid making the logo too jokey; the comedy is stronger when the brand takes itself seriously.
GUARDRAILS
20  DO / DON’T MASTER SHEET

DO
■ Think “future imagined in 2001.”
■ Use white, navy, cobalt and cyan with restraint.
■ Keep architecture clean and massively shaped.
■ Use fashion-like angular character silhouettes.
■ Simplify character shading into designed planes.
■ Preserve cross-window readability.
■ Let boring office objects coexist with futuristic design.
■ Use signage as part of architecture.
■ Keep corporate/legal humour straight-faced.
■ Make UI and world graphics feel like one brand system.
■ Prefer one memorable shape over ten small details.
DON’T
■ Drift into vaporwave or pastel Y2K.
■ Add greenery/bubbles just to signal Frutiger Aero.
■ Use neon cyberpunk as the default atmosphere.
■ Make the cast cute, chibi or baby-faced.
■ Apply thick black outlines to everything.
■ Fill characters with complex toon-shader effects.
■ Cover walls in random sci-fi panels and pipes.
■ Use dirt and scratches as the main realism cue.
■ Hide players behind reflective glass or visual clutter.
■ Overdesign every office prop.
■ Let procedural layouts break the visual hierarchy.

Instant rejection checklist
■ Would this still look like the project if the blue accent were removed? If not, the design may be relying on colour instead of shape language.
■ Does the asset need a paragraph to explain why it is futuristic? If yes, simplify the design idea.
■ Does the character look cute before they look sharp? Push angles, length and facial restraint.
■ Does the environment look like generic cyberpunk with white paint? Remove clutter and rethink massing.
■ Does the shader impress technically but obscure the graphic silhouette? Remove passes until the character reads again.

Character
■ Jet Set Radio-like long-limbed proportions, but less cute.
